# Original pilot lesson 01 — The Museum of Unsent Letters

**Status:** original, provisional reading-to-writing lesson. **Not an official Cambridge lesson.**  
**Time:** approximately 50–60 minutes.  
**Skills:** interpretation, perspective, narrative voice, structure, evidence-based explanation, purposeful revision.

## Today’s question

**Can silence tell the truth, or can it hide it?**

## 1. Retrieve (5 minutes)

If a prior diagnostic is available, ask the student to explain the difference between **an observation** and **an inference**. If no prior work is visible, explain both terms briefly using a fresh example, then ask the student to invent one.

## 2. Read (10–15 minutes)

### The Museum of Unsent Letters

The museum occupied three rooms above a bakery. It had no famous paintings, no dinosaur bones and no gift shop. Its most valuable objects were envelopes that had never been opened.

Every envelope had been written by someone in the town of Alderwick and then, for one reason or another, never sent. Some were apologies. Others were declarations of love, resignation letters or complaints about neighbours' dogs. The museum kept the letters in glass drawers, with the names of their writers hidden behind little brass plates.

Visitors could read the dates, the addresses and the first line of each letter. They could never read the rest.

Mira had worked there for two years and had become skilled at explaining the rules.

“People come here,” she told school groups, “because an unfinished message can reveal as much as a finished one.”

She said this so often that it began to sound like something printed on the wall. She had never been certain it was true.

One wet afternoon, an elderly visitor arrived carrying a narrow blue envelope. He placed it on the counter with both hands.

“I wrote this fifty years ago,” he said. “I would like it added to the collection.”

The envelope was addressed to a woman named Elian Rose. The first line read: *I know what happened on the bridge, and I should have spoken sooner.*

Mira looked up.

“What happened on the bridge?”

“That is the part I never sent.”

He smiled, but the smile did not settle comfortably on his face.

Museum policy required an unsent letter to remain sealed. Mira explained this, then asked whether the writer wanted to leave any context for future visitors.

“No,” he said. “I want it to stay a question.”

After he left, Mira placed the envelope in an empty drawer. The rain continued against the windows. Below, the bakery shut its doors, and the smell of warm bread faded from the stairwell.

Mira looked at the brass plate that would cover the writer's name. The museum had always seemed to her a place that protected privacy. Now, for the first time, she wondered whether it sometimes protected people from responsibility.

She reached for the label printer. The standard label would read: *Unsent letter, 1976. Donated by its author.*

Instead, she typed: *A letter whose recipient never had the chance to answer.*

She held the label between her fingers for a long moment.

Then she put it on the drawer.

## 3. Analyse (10–15 minutes)

Ask **one** of the following at a time. Require a claim, a brief quotation and reasoning.

1. How does Mira's understanding of the museum change? Identify the turning point.
2. Why does the writer want the letter to “stay a question”? Give **two plausible interpretations** and say which is better supported.
3. What is the effect of the repeated emphasis on sealed envelopes, first lines and hidden names?
4. Compare the standard label and Mira's replacement label. How does each frame the same object differently?

If the student is stuck, point to the relevant paragraph before giving a larger hint. Do not supply the assessed response.

## 4. Write (15–20 minutes)

**Choose one:**

**A. Argument (180–250 words):** Should the museum accept letters whose contents may concern wrongdoing? Give a reasoned position, consider a counterargument and use details from the passage.

**B. Creative writing (180–250 words):** Write an unsent letter from a person who witnessed an event on the bridge. The letter must imply a moral dilemma without simply explaining everything. Create a deliberate voice and ending.

First produce a **three-point outline**. Then write **Draft 1 independently**.

## 5. Revise and reflect (5–10 minutes)

The tutor gives feedback on **one rubric criterion** with an exact example from the draft, and asks for a student-authored revision. The student explains **what changed and why**.

Finish with a session record: observed reading skill, one writing strength, one growth target, amount of help received and a suggested retrieval question for next time.

## Accessibility / format notes

- Desktop: keep passage visible beside the answer; on mobile, offer a way to reopen it.
- Do not use a forced timer. Allow a shorter version when needed.
- If the tutor cannot see the text, it must request this file rather than inventing a different passage.
